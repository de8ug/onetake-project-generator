# Two Editorial Lenses

Use this before packaging and after reading the complete draft aloud internally. Prefer a few concrete revision decisions to a ritual scorecard.

## Operator: Why Would A Stranger Care?

- Name one audience and their actual question. A broad topic like AI needs a specific tension: "I finish tasks faster but still feel busy", not "the future of AI".
- The cover and first sentences promise the same answer. Avoid fear, unverifiable rankings, mandatory urgency, a fake free gift or a deliberately hidden advertisement.
- Establish relevance before background. "You open the camera and forget the next line" needs no project knowledge; "we updated this pipeline" does.
- Deliver a useful distinction or small test the viewer can apply without buying anything. Not every piece needs instructions, but even a personal reflection should change how they see the question.
- A closing can leave space or ask one meaningful question. Do not require comments, follow/like or an interaction hook on every piece.
- In a batch, vary tensions, examples and endings. Select public-interest AI/work/education topics when requested, rather than six disguised product introductions.

## Creator: Can A Person Actually Say This?

- Start from a recognizable scene, question or contrast. Not every opening must be first-person, and not every scene must be presented as a true personal memory.
- Give the viewer a concrete example before naming the abstraction. Explain "attention" through choosing among three returned drafts, not a sequence of management terms.
- Test causal claims: faster execution can lead to more tasks **if the person opens more tasks**; it does not prove everyone becomes busier.
- Write short spoken turns. Replace "next I analyze three dimensions" only when the replacement really fits the speaker; avoid mechanical fillers and an imposed imitation of another creator.
- Earn the highlighted line. A quote should summarize something already being explained, not be a decorative maxim on every segment. Allow quote-free openings and human pauses.
- Keep the ending proportionate to the argument. Do not suddenly turn an educational discussion into a course pitch or a product catalogue.

## Whiteboard Test

Each board must answer why this is easier to see than to hear: parallel columns, a changed bottleneck, a branching task, a before/after path. Titles alone do not count as visual explanation. Keep board text far shorter than narration. Use editable words/shapes, not flattened cover images. Constrain split-view essentials to the upper half and keep PIP away from critical content.

## Talking-Head Test

Remove B-roll: is the spoken story still understandable? Keep the opening with the person when that is the selected style. Use an actual screenshot only to prove the relevant claim, not to add movement. Do not replace the speaker with a generated person. Gold/yellow emphasis is for a few important words; captions are semantic summaries, not speech-recognition subtitles.

## Grounding Ledger

Separate verified sources, personal judgments, explicitly hypothetical examples, and unconfirmed material. Numbers in a diagram must say "示意" when they are examples rather than measurements. Never inherit another draft's unsupported "I did this for ten years" or "children improved". Do not claim real interface screenshots when the visual is a schematic. Unknown publication performance stays unknown.

## Release Review

Check novice comprehension, speakability, complete payoff, source honesty, visual purpose and disclosure. Any unresolved blocker stays outside the spoken assertions. Parser success, a static preview and a fresh live recording are separate evidence levels. A human should still revise words into their own voice and approve publication.
